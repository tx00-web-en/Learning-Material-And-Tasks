# Theory: DEEP & INVEST

## From Last Week to This Week

Last week, you explored Scrum by reverse-engineering Airbnb.

You identified users, created user stories, built a Product Backlog, prioritized backlog items, selected a Sprint Backlog, defined a Sprint Goal, and discussed how a Scrum team works during a Sprint.

This week, we look at what happens **after a Product Backlog has been created**.

A Product Backlog is not a finished list.

As the team learns more about the product, users, and the problem they are trying to solve, the backlog changes.

Some items become clearer.

Some items become more important.

Some items become less important.

Some items need to be split.

Some new items appear.

Some items may no longer be needed.

So before planning a future Sprint, the team needs to ask:

> **Is our Product Backlog healthy?**

And:

> **Are the individual backlog items good enough to work on?**

Two useful ideas help us answer these questions:

# DEEP and INVEST

---

# 1. DEEP — A Healthy Product Backlog

**DEEP** describes four characteristics of a good Product Backlog:

* **D — Detailed appropriately**
* **E — Estimated**
* **E — Emergent**
* **P — Prioritized**

DEEP is about looking at the **Product Backlog as a whole**.

Think of DEEP as a way to inspect the backlog before making decisions about future Sprints.

---

## D — Detailed Appropriately

Not every item in the Product Backlog needs the same amount of detail.

Items that are likely to be worked on soon should contain enough information for the team to understand and discuss them.

Items that are further down the backlog can remain less detailed.

For example:

### Near the top of the backlog

> As a customer, I want to receive an order confirmation after placing an order, so that I know my order was successfully received.

This item may need:

* a clear description
* acceptance criteria
* an estimate
* discussion about what "confirmation" means

### Further down the backlog

> As a customer, I want to save favourite restaurants, so that I can find them more easily later.

This might not need detailed specifications yet.

Why?

Because the team may not work on it for several Sprints.

By the time they get to it, they may have learned new information and the story may change.

### The important idea

> **More detail is not always better.**

The goal is:

> **The right amount of detail at the right time.**

---

# 2. E — Estimated

Backlog items should have some understanding of their relative effort.

Teams often use **story points** to estimate backlog items.

Story points do not represent exact hours.

Instead, they help the team compare the relative:

* effort
* complexity
* risk
* uncertainty

of different items.

For example:

| Story                      | Estimate |
| -------------------------- | -------: |
| Display restaurant details |        3 |
| Send order confirmation    |        3 |
| Process payment            |        5 |
| Complete checkout          |       13 |

A 13-point story is not necessarily "four times as much time" as a 3-point story.

The numbers help the team communicate about the **relative size and complexity** of the work.

### Important question

If the team cannot understand what a story actually means, can they estimate it confidently?

Usually, a story needs more discussion or refinement first.

---

# 3. E — Emergent

A Product Backlog is **not static**.

It changes as the team learns.

For example, imagine that QuickBite's team discovers:

> "Customers are abandoning checkout because they cannot see delivery costs before placing an order."

This is new information.

The Product Owner and team may decide to:

* add a new backlog item
* change an existing story
* change priorities
* split an item
* remove something that is no longer important

The Product Backlog therefore **emerges over time**.

This is important because we cannot know everything about the product at the beginning.

### Remember

> **The Product Backlog is a living, evolving list.**

Do not spend a lot of time perfectly specifying work that may change before the team ever develops it.

---

# 4. P — Prioritized

The Product Backlog should be ordered according to what is most important.

The Product Owner is responsible for ordering the Product Backlog, considering factors such as:

* customer value
* business value
* importance
* risk
* dependencies
* new information

Imagine a Product Owner says:

> "Everything is HIGH priority."

That does not help the team decide what to work on first.

A useful backlog should help answer:

> **"If we can only work on a few things next, which things should we choose?"**

For example:

| Priority | Backlog Item               |
| -------- | -------------------------- |
| 1        | Place an order             |
| 2        | Pay for an order           |
| 3        | Receive order confirmation |
| 4        | Cancel an order            |
| 5        | Save favourite restaurants |

The exact order will depend on the product and the decisions of the Product Owner.

The important point is that the backlog should be **ordered**, not simply a collection of items.

---

# DEEP in One Picture

Think about DEEP as asking four questions about the **whole Product Backlog**:

| DEEP                           | Question                                               |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| **D — Detailed appropriately** | Do important/near-term items have enough detail?       |
| **E — Estimated**              | Do we have useful estimates?                           |
| **E — Emergent**               | Can the backlog evolve as we learn?                    |
| **P — Prioritized**            | Is the backlog ordered according to what matters most? |

So:

> **DEEP = Product Backlog health**

---

# 5. INVEST — A Healthy Backlog Item

DEEP looks at the whole Product Backlog.

Now we zoom in.

Take **one user story**.

Is this particular story good enough for the team to work with?

The **INVEST** criteria help us think about that.

INVEST stands for:

* **I — Independent**
* **N — Negotiable**
* **V — Valuable**
* **E — Estimable**
* **S — Small**
* **T — Testable**

---

# I — Independent

A good user story should be as independent as reasonably possible from other stories.

For example, imagine we have:

> Story A: As a customer, I want to add a meal to my cart.

> Story B: As a customer, I want to place an order from my cart.

Story B depends to some extent on the ability to have a cart.

Dependencies are not automatically proof that a story is "bad".

Instead, the team should ask:

> **Can we structure the work so that stories can be developed and delivered with as little unnecessary dependency as possible?**

Independence gives the team more flexibility when planning and ordering work.

---

# N — Negotiable

A user story is **not a detailed technical contract**.

The story describes the need and value, but the details can be discussed.

For example:

> As a customer, I want to receive an order confirmation so that I know my order was successfully received.

This does not necessarily specify:

* exactly how the confirmation looks
* whether it is an email or notification
* the exact UI design
* the technical implementation

Those details can be discussed between the Product Owner and Developers.

### Remember

> **The story describes what is valuable; the details can be negotiated.**

---

# V — Valuable

A good story should provide value to a user, customer, stakeholder, or the product.

Compare:

### Weak

> As a customer, I want a blue button.

This describes a UI element, but the value is unclear.

### Better

> As a customer, I want to place my order, so that I can purchase my food.

The second story describes a user outcome.

Ask:

> **Who benefits from this?**

and:

> **What problem does it solve?**

If nobody can explain the value, the story deserves more discussion.

---

# E — Estimable

The team should understand the story well enough to estimate it.

Consider:

> As a customer, I want the best possible ordering experience.

How big is that?

Nobody really knows.

Compare:

> As a customer, I want to receive an order confirmation after placing an order.

This is much easier to discuss and estimate.

If a story is difficult to estimate, ask:

> **Do we understand what we are actually building?**

The problem might be that the story is:

* too vague
* too large
* missing important information
* uncertain
* hiding several different pieces of work

---

# S — Small

A story should be small enough to be completed within a Sprint.

Consider:

> As a restaurant owner, I want to manage my restaurant profile, menu, prices, opening hours, delivery areas, promotions, and photos.

This is probably too much for one story.

We could consider splitting it into smaller user-focused stories.

For example:

> As a restaurant owner, I want to update my restaurant information, so that customers see accurate information.

> As a restaurant owner, I want to manage my menu, so that customers can see the food I offer.

> As a restaurant owner, I want to update my opening hours, so that customers know when I am available.

### Important

Do not split a story simply because it contains several technical tasks.

The goal is to create **smaller valuable pieces of work**, not just smaller technical tasks.

---

# T — Testable

A good story should allow the team to determine whether it has been successfully completed.

Consider:

> As a customer, I want the app to be beautiful.

How do we know when this is done?

It is difficult to test objectively.

Compare:

> As a customer, I want to see the total cost of my order before confirming payment, so that I know how much I will pay.

This gives us something we can verify.

We can define acceptance criteria such as:

* the total is displayed
* the total includes the selected items
* delivery cost is included
* the customer can see the total before confirming payment

### Remember

> **If we cannot explain how we would test it, the story probably needs more discussion.**

---

# INVEST in One Picture

When looking at an **individual backlog item**, ask:

| INVEST              | Question                                                             |
| ------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **I — Independent** | Can this be developed/delivered with limited unnecessary dependency? |
| **N — Negotiable**  | Can the details be discussed and refined?                            |
| **V — Valuable**    | Is the user/customer value clear?                                    |
| **E — Estimable**   | Does the team understand it well enough to estimate it?              |
| **S — Small**       | Is it small enough for a Sprint?                                     |
| **T — Testable**    | Can we determine whether it is complete?                             |

So:

> **INVEST = individual backlog item health**

---

# 6. DEEP vs INVEST

This distinction is important.

Imagine we are looking at the QuickBite Product Backlog.

### Looking at the whole backlog:

> Are the items appropriately detailed?

> Are they estimated?

> Can the backlog change?

> Are they prioritized?

We are thinking about:

# DEEP

---

### Looking at one story:

> Is it Independent?

> Is it Negotiable?

> Is it Valuable?

> Is it Estimable?

> Is it Small?

> Is it Testable?

We are thinking about:

# INVEST

**DEEP**

↓

**INVEST**

↓

# 7. DEEP and INVEST Work Together

DEEP and INVEST are not competing ideas.

They work together.

Imagine this Product Backlog:

| Priority | Story                      | Points |
| -------- | -------------------------- | -----: |
| 1        | Place an order             |      8 |
| 2        | Complete checkout          |     13 |
| 3        | Receive order confirmation |      3 |
| 4        | Save favourite restaurants |      5 |

The backlog might be **prioritized and estimated**.

That is part of DEEP.

But then we inspect:

> **"Complete checkout"**

and discover that it actually includes:

* entering delivery information
* selecting payment
* calculating delivery cost
* confirming the order
* displaying confirmation

It might not be **Small**.

It might also be difficult to **Estimate** and **Test**.

So we improve the individual story.

For example, we might split it into smaller valuable stories.

This is how DEEP and INVEST work together.

---

# 8. A Backlog Is Not Perfect

A common misunderstanding is:

> **"We need to make the entire Product Backlog perfect before starting development."**

That is not the goal.

A healthy backlog evolves.

Items near the top generally need more attention because they are more likely to be developed soon.

Items further down can remain less detailed because priorities and understanding may change.

So the goal is not:

> **Perfect backlog**

The goal is:

> **A backlog that supports good decisions.**

---

# 9. From Backlog to Sprint

Eventually, the team needs to decide:

> **What should we work on next?**

This is where the ideas come together.

The Product Backlog contains many possible items.

The team considers:

* priority
* value
* dependencies
* estimates
* available capacity
* whether items are understood well enough
* whether the items are small enough
* whether they can be tested
* the Sprint Goal

Then the team selects work for the Sprint Backlog.

---

# The Big Question

When preparing for a Sprint, do not simply ask:

> **"Which stories have the highest priority?"**

Ask:

> **"Which valuable items are ready enough for us to make a good Sprint decision?"**

This is the thinking you will practice in today's activity.

**DEEP**

* Can it change?
* Is it prioritized?

### About individual stories

**INVEST**

* Is it Independent?
* Is it Negotiable?
* Is it Valuable?
* Is it Estimable?
* Is it Small?
* Is it Testable?

---

# 11. What Happens When New Information Arrives?

Imagine that during Sprint 1, the team discovers:

> **Customers are abandoning checkout because delivery costs are unclear.**

What should happen?

The answer is not necessarily:

> "Ignore the backlog because Sprint 1 is finished."

Instead, the team should inspect what has been learned.

The Product Backlog may change.

Perhaps:

* an existing item becomes more important
* a new backlog item is added
* another item moves down
* an existing story is rewritten
* a story is split
* an item becomes unnecessary

This is **Emergent**.

The backlog reflects what the team currently understands.

# 12. From Theory to Practice

In today's activity, you will become the team responsible for **QuickBite's Sprint 2**.

Your Product Backlog contains problems.

You will receive **Trouble Cards** that introduce new situations.

You will have a limited **Scrum Budget**.

Your team will need to:

### FIND

Find the problem.

### EXPLAIN

### FIX

### DEFEND

Explain why your decision makes sense.

### DECIDE

Choose what you would take into Sprint 2.

---

# Final Takeaway

Remember these three questions:

### 1. Looking at the whole backlog:

> **Is our Product Backlog DEEP?**

### 2. Looking at one story:

> **Is this backlog item INVEST?**

### 3. Preparing for a Sprint:

> **Would we take this item into the next Sprint?**

That is the connection:

# DEEP → INVEST → Sprint Decision

**Healthy Backlog → Healthy Stories → Better Sprint Decisions**
